# Day 1 — Company Rule (1773–1858)

**Date:** 1 November 2026  
**Course:** UPSC CSE Indian Polity Master Course  
**Module:** 1 — Historical Background and Making of the Constitution  
**Session status:** Teaching delivered; no assessment requested or attempted.

## Scope taught

- East India Company: commercial origins and transformation into a territorial power.
- Battle of Plassey (1757), Battle of Buxar (1764), and grant of Diwani (1765).
- Dual Government in Bengal: Diwani and Nizamat.
- Regulating Act, 1773: Governor-General of Bengal, executive council, subordination of Bombay and Madras in specified matters, Supreme Court at Calcutta (established 1774), and restrictions on Company officials.
- Pitt's India Act, 1784: Board of Control, Court of Directors, dual control and stronger British governmental supervision.
- Charter Act, 1813: end of Company trade monopoly in India except tea and trade with China; education provision and missionary entry.
- Charter Act, 1833: end of remaining Company commercial activities, Governor-General of India, legislative centralisation, Law Member and Law Commission.
- Charter Act, 1853: legislative-executive differentiation, additional legislative members and principle of open competition in civil-service recruitment.

## Key factual anchors taught

- First Governor-General of Bengal: Warren Hastings.
- Supreme Court at Calcutta established in 1774; first Chief Justice: Sir Elijah Impey.
- Pitt's India Act established the Board of Control and system of dual control.
- First Governor-General of India: Lord William Bentinck.
- First Law Member: Lord Macaulay.
- Macaulay Committee appointed in 1854 following the 1853 Act's open-competition principle.

## Examination application

Prelims distinctions covered: 1773 versus 1784; 1813 versus 1833; Governor-General of Bengal versus Governor-General of India; provision for Supreme Court versus its establishment; 1853 open-competition principle versus later recruitment arrangements.

Mains application discussed: colonial institutional evolution as a foundation for later constitutional development, while distinguishing colonial administrative objectives from democratic self-government.
